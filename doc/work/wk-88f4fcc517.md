## detail

THE OWNER'S WORDS: we can discuss how we deal with long time statistics without storing every single item, especially the ephemerals. I do not necessarily want to store them. I guess retro should produce a statistic for its own thing, and they should be published. THE TENSION, AND IT IS THE WHOLE QUESTION. A burndown and a failure rate are about a long run, and the things they are computed from are ephemeral by design: the log rotates, the scratchpad drains, and se retro exists to take both away so nothing is counted twice. A statistic computed from what is still there answers only about what has not been swept yet. THE SHAPE THAT MAY WORK, and it wants agreeing before it is built. A retro closes a period, so a retro is where the period's numbers are fixed: it counts what it is about to take, writes the counts into its folder and into something that survives the drain, and publishes them. The long run is then a list of periods rather than a heap of items, and it costs one small file per retro instead of every ephemeral forever. WHAT IS UNCERTAIN. Where the surviving file lives, since the scratchpad and the log are both drained and doc is version control. Whether a retro that is run twice in a day makes two periods or one. And what published means: a file in the tree, a line in the panel, or both. THE BURNDOWN TOKEN DEPENDS ON THIS ANSWER, wk-1b7c1a2da1, which asks the engine for minted per day, done per day, open plus backlogged, and the review failure rate per day. Its per-day numbers have to come from somewhere that outlives a drain, and this token is where that somewhere is decided. AND WHICH IMPROVEMENT DID IT, WHICH IS THE OWNER'S SECOND HALF. Their words: if we look at the Git history and the history at which the failures we choose, we can find out which improvements were how impactful. Git carries the dates and the notes carry the rounds, so the failure rate can be cut at every commit that touched doc/guidance and read off either side. That turns a lesson from something we believed into something we measured, and it says when a rule has stopped earning its place. IT ALSO ANSWERS THE OPPOSITE QUESTION, which nothing here asks today: whether a rule we wrote made anything better. The method has grown every week and no measurement has ever been taken against it. WHAT IS UNCERTAIN, and it belongs with the rest of this token. Whether a period is cut at a commit or at a retro, and how a rate over a handful of tokens is read at all, because the sample either side of one commit is small and a difference in it may be noise. Say what counts as a difference before looking, so the answer is not chosen after the fact.
