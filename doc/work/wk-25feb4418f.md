IT REFUSED ONE, saying to move its files one at a time. Renaming source to src

and replacing that loop is the whole point of the verb.

THE STATED REASON WAS WRONG TOO. It said moving a folder would leave every

TWO RULES, AND EACH FAILS WITHOUT THE OTHER.

path under it changes and the English word does not.

A prefix expects a word after it. The rule that stops doc/old.md matching
inside doc/old.mdx looks at the character after the match and refuses a letter,
which is every path under a folder.

the rename is a test of the verb, and a failure is a finding on the verb rather

## lesson 1 · round 1 · by reviewer

**the class:** A pattern narrowed to avoid a false positive in the ambiguous case, then carried unchanged into the case that was never ambiguous. Adding the separator was right where the folder's name is also an English word; kept for a nested path, where the name cannot be anything but a path, the narrowing quietly drops the commonest form of the thing being matched. What hides it is that the verb's report of what it could not reach is computed from the same narrowed rule that did the missing, so the gap reports itself as zero and the caller is told there is nothing left to do.

**instead:** When you narrow a rule to dodge a false positive, write down what the narrowing now misses and check whether the reason for it holds in every case the rule covers. A reason that applies to the top of a tree and not to the rest of it is two rules, not one. And never compute what a verb missed with the rule that did the missing: measure the residue against the widest spelling of the thing, so anything the narrow rule skipped is named out loud rather than counted as none.
